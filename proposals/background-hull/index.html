GoFish API proposal · background / hull

One enclose: a box, or a blob with hull and spine shortcuts

GoFish already has a box-only enclose (also exported as background). This proposal turns it into one relational mark that draws an outline around a set of nodes: a box, or a smooth region built from discs around the members and links between them. The outlines are values of a new strategy family, Shape, chosen with a shape option the way Curve is chosen with curve on line. The region member, Shape.blob(), fills the space between members with the alpha shape's rule (dialed by convexity), links them with a spanning tree so the set stays in one piece, and rounds the outline so its curvature never jumps. Two knobs shape it, and they do not overlap: convexity alone decides what is filled, and roundness changes only how sharply the outline bends, never filling a gap, opening a hole or joining two pieces. The convex hull and the spine are shortcuts of it, the way sina() and jitter() are shortcuts of noise(). A member can be any placed shape, so a graph's edges can be members next to its nodes. The design idea is a discrete choice followed by continuous tuning: pick a shape from a small set (box, hull, blob, spine), then adjust the blob's parameters to taste.

Recommendation. Rebuild enclose with createRelationalMark and give it shape: Shape.box({ rx, ry }) | Shape.blob({ convexity, roundness, neck, avoiding, clearance }), with two shortcuts: Shape.hull(opts) = Shape.blob({ convexity: 1, neck: 1, ...opts }) and Shape.spine(opts) = Shape.blob({ convexity: 0, ...opts }). padding and paint stay on enclose. Delete the background alias. Do not add a .background() modifier yet; it is the runner-up, to add later as exact sugar.

Part 3, History, holds the earlier versions and the retired decisions, collapsed. Every picture is a real render of a throwaway prototype story (stories/_proposals/BackgroundHull.stories.tsx). Part 1 · The current proposal

Terms

Box, convex hull

The box is the smallest rectangle, sides parallel to the axes, that holds the members; enclose draws it today. The convex hull is the smallest convex shape that holds them: the straight line between any two of its points stays inside, so it never has dents.

Member, non-member, hyperedge

Members are the nodes a shape must contain; non-members are the nodes it must keep out. A hyperedge joins any number of nodes, so a set drawn as one region is a hyperedge.

Relational mark, strategy family

A relational mark takes its geometry from already placed nodes, like line. A strategy family is a capitalized namespace of option values, such as Curve.monotone() (#1013, one uppercase namespace per family).

Padding, spine

Padding is the distance in pixels between the members and the outline (#1005 settled it as the inset around the outer edge). A spine is a tree of links joining the members, drawn as a smooth tube; bubble sets (Collins et al., 2009) are built this way.

Delaunay, spanning tree, alpha shape

The Delaunay triangulation splits points into triangles with no point inside any triangle's circumcircle. The minimum spanning tree is the shortest tree joining all members. The alpha shape fills the Delaunay triangles whose circumradius is at most alpha.

Closing, blur, curvature

A morphological closing grows a shape by s and shrinks it back, which rounds dents and fills gaps narrower than about 2s; the current construction does not use it. A Gaussian blur averages nearby values with bell-curve weights; the current construction smooths the outline this way along its own length. Curvature is 1 over the radius of the best-fitting circle; an outline is G2 when its curvature never jumps.

Topology, sensitivity matrix

Topology here is how many pieces an outline has and how many holes. A sensitivity matrix has one row per measured property of the outline and one column per knob; each cell says how much that knob alone moves the property, from 0 (not at all) to 1 (as much as it ever moves).

The syntax

JS
// one hull per species: group the points in a second layer
chart(penguins)
  .flow(scatter({ x: "Beak Length (mm)", y: "Beak Depth (mm)" }))
  .mark(circle({ r: 3, fill: "Species" }).name("points"))
  .layer(
    chart(selectAll("points"))
      .flow(group({ by: "Species" }))
      .mark(enclose({ shape: Shape.hull(), padding: 6,
                      fill: "Species", opacity: 0.2 })))

// contains a and c but not b: combinator form over refs
layer([a, b, c]).relate(({ a, b, c }) => [
  enclose({ shape: Shape.spine({ avoiding: [b], neck: 0.85,
                                 clearance: 6 }),
            padding: 22, fill: "#ff2400" }, [a, c]),
])

Members and presets

Value Is exactly Draws
Shape.box(opts) its own member; options rx, ry the padded bounding box with rounded corners; the default shape
Shape.blob(opts) the general region discs, links and filled triangles, rounded (below)
Shape.hull(opts) Shape.blob({ convexity: 1, neck: 1, ...opts }) the convex hull grown by padding, rounded (within half a pixel)
Shape.spine(opts) Shape.blob({ convexity: 0, ...opts }) the routed spanning tree of tubes and nothing else, rounded

As sina() and jitter() do over noise() (#1018, which made the scatter overlap strategy one noise() with two presets), a shortcut makes the same object as the general call (kind: "blob"), declared once as a preset, and any option overrides the preset. So Shape.hull({ avoiding: others }) is legal and draws the hull with each other node inside it cut out as a round hole, and Shape.hull({ roundness: 15 }) is legal and draws a hull with softer corners. Shape.box() stays separate: a box is a rectangle grown by padding, not a union of discs.

So the four values are a small discrete menu, and the blob's parameters are the continuous dials behind three of them. A user picks the shape that reads best first (the penguins tab shows the four side by side), and only then tunes convexity, roundness and neck.

Parameters

Parameter On Meaning Direction Default
padding enclose The bulge around each member, from the member's own outline (for a line, around its whole length). Exact for a member on its own. Bigger is fatter. as today
fill, stroke, … enclose Paint, shared by every member of the family. as today
rx, ry Shape.box Corner rounding, as on rect. Bigger is rounder. 0
convexity Shape.blob How much of the space between members is filled, from 0 (a spanning tree of links) to 1 (every Delaunay triangle: the convex hull). Unitless. It is the only parameter that fills gaps, so it alone decides the pieces, the holes and the filled area. Bigger is fuller and more convex. 0.5
roundness Shape.blob How far the outline's corners are rounded, in pixels (the smoothing width σ is half of it). It changes curvature only: the pieces and holes stay the same, and the filled area moves by a few percent at most. It takes effect up to the smallest circle in the region: a member's grown radius, a link's half-width, a box's corner, or a keep-out circle. Bigger is rounder. same as padding
neck Shape.blob Width of links that do not bound a filled triangle, as a fraction of the bulge. 0 to 1. Bigger is thicker; 1 is as thick. Above 1 is an error: a tube wider than the bulge swallows it, so the knob would only repeat padding, unevenly. 1
avoiding Shape.blob Nodes to keep out. Each listed node that is not a member is avoided, so the user never computes a set difference. none
clearance Shape.blob Gap in pixels kept from each avoided node. Bigger keeps farther away. 4

How the blob is built

Shape.blob() draws one set in six steps, after layout, in screen space (so it works under polar and other coordinate transforms the way curves do).

  1. Skeleton. Each member is its own geometry: a node is its center, and a line member (such as a graph edge) is its segment, which already links its two ends. Take the Delaunay triangulation of the members' points (node centers and line ends). Fill every triangle whose circumradius is at most alpha, whatever it holds. The links are the edges of the filled triangles, the other Delaunay edges no longer than 2 alpha that no avoided node blocks, and the minimum spanning tree over the members, routed around the avoided nodes (always, so the set stays in one piece). Where a routed link wraps an avoided node, it follows that node's keep-out circle exactly: the route is straight lines tangent to the circles and arcs of them. A spanning-tree link runs between the closest points of two members' geometries, so members that touch, like an edge and its end node, are joined at no cost.
  2. Pieces. Each member grown by padding (its bulge, radius R; for a line member, a tube around its whole length). Each link that bounds a filled triangle is a tube as wide as the bulge; every other link is a tube neck times the bulge. The filled triangles. The union of these pieces is the region, and nothing else fills it.
  3. Cut. Each avoided node's keep-out zone (the node grown by clearance) is cut out of the region. A zone inside the region becomes a hole that is exactly that circle. A zone across the region's edge becomes a round notch.
  4. Exact outline. The outline of what is left is traced exactly. Every piece and every zone has a distance in closed form (a disc, a tube, a box, a triangle, or a tube bent around an avoided node), so each outline point is found to a ten-thousandth of a pixel, on a 1 px grid that is searched only near the outline. Each point records the circle it lies on, if any. This step does not depend on roundness.
  5. Rounding. The outline is smoothed along its own length with a Gaussian of width σ = roundness / 2. Smoothing pulls a circle of radius r toward its center by a known factor (about σ²/(2r)), so first every point that lies on a circle is moved out from that circle's center by the same factor. Circles and straight sides then come back exactly where they were, and only corners and joins change: they round. So a lone member ends exactly at padding, a hole is exactly its clearance circle, and the outline is curvature-continuous, because a smoothed curve has derivatives of every order. Two cases need more. Smoothing along the outline cannot reach across a narrow inlet, whose two sides are close in the plane but far apart along the outline, so an inlet narrower than 1.5 σ is closed first. And smoothing fills corners a little, so each point near a keep-out zone is moved out by a smooth maximum of its gap and 0; the outline never enters a zone.
  6. Topology. Smoothing moves each traced loop on its own, so the pieces and holes are the traced ones at every roundness. If two loops, or two parts of one, would cross, σ is narrowed by bisection until they do not. Rounding also stops at the smallest circle in the region (a member's bulge, a link's half-width, a box corner, a keep-out circle), because rounding further would bend that circle.

Holes and detours. Step 1 decides whether an avoided node becomes a hole or the outline goes around it, and the user does not choose. A node inside a filled triangle is inside the region, so it becomes a hole. A node that no filled triangle covers stays outside, and the links route around it. In the Topology example, a and c form no triangle, so b is never inside, and the band passes under b at every convexity.

The convexity scale. alpha = m × convexity / (1 − convexity), where m is the median link length of the members' minimum spanning tree (a typical distance between neighbors). So 0 fills nothing, 0.5 fills triangles up to about the typical spacing, and 1 fills everything. Measuring alpha in units of m makes the rule depend only on the shape of the point set, not on the chart's size.

Why these knobs are orthogonal

Josh found that convexity and the old smoothing overlapped. They did, because both filled gaps. convexity fills the Delaunay triangles whose circumradius is at most alpha. The old smoothing was a morphological closing by a radius s, and on discs of radius R a closing fills the same triangles as an alpha shape with alpha = R + s, because the closing's disc fits in a triangle's gap exactly when that triangle's empty circle is wider than R + s. So the two knobs ran one operation twice at two scales. The blur, the only step that changed curvature alone, had no knob of its own, since its width was half of smoothing.

Now convexity decides topology and filled area, and roundness decides only curvature. The closing is gone, so every fill comes from convexity's triangles, and the rounding is the roundness knob.

To check this, we measured eight properties of the outline over a full grid of the knobs: convexity at 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1; the second knob at 0, ½, 1 and 2 times the padding; neck at 0.5, 0.75 and 1; and padding and clearance at three values each. We used four datasets: the U, the Gentoo penguins with the other species avoided, Topology, and the hyperedges with edges as members. The properties are these.

  • Fill. The area beyond the padded spanning tree, as a share of the padded convex hull.
  • Gap. The radius of the widest empty disc inside the padded hull that is neither filled nor a keep-out zone.
  • Radius. The smallest radius of curvature on the outline (capped at 40 px).
  • Padding. The measured distance from the outermost members to the outline.
  • Neck. The narrowest link width, as a share of the bulge.
  • Clearance. The measured gap at avoided nodes the outline comes near.
  • Hole shape. A hole's area divided by its keep-out circle's area, where 1 is an exact circle.
  • Topology. Whether the number of pieces or holes changes.

Each cell is the average, over all settings of the other knobs, of how far the property moves along this knob alone, divided by how far it moves over the whole grid. For topology, the cell is the share of sweeps along which the count changes. The four datasets are averaged. Darker cells move more.

The rows that move with padding, clearance and neck are expected. Those knobs are distances, and a distance can join two pieces or close a hole, so the claim is about the two shape knobs only.

Before, smoothing moved the filled area (0.19), the hole shape (0.23) and the topology (along 48% of its sweeps), and convexity moved the smallest radius (0.23), so each knob did part of the other's job. After, roundness changes the topology along none of its sweeps (0.00) and the hole shape hardly at all (0.03). Its largest effect is on the radius (0.21), which is its job. Its smaller effects on fill and clearance are the bit of area that rounding a concave corner adds, and the lips of a notch moving as they round. convexity still moves the radius, because filling triangles makes new corners, so that is the shape changing, not a second rounding control. The hole shape now depends on clearance alone (0.89).

Custom shapes and the ellipse

Josh's ellipse fits the #1101 escape hatch (one extensibility model for strategy families, still being designed): a user function in place of a Shape value. It receives the members as { box, boundary } in pixels after placement, the box of all members, the avoided nodes, and padding, and returns a closed outline as a list of points. enclose still paints it below the members and uses its box. The Munkres panel in the explorer draws its ellipses this way. Shape.ellipse() can become a member later if ellipses turn out to be common.

JS
const oval = ({ box, padding }) =>
  ellipsePoints(box, padding); // user code
enclose({ shape: oval, padding: 30 }, [a, b]);
Python
enclose([a, b],
        shape=lambda s: ellipse_points(s.box, s.padding),
        padding=30)

What the user writes

The data is two tables: edges rows { id, source, target } and memberships rows { set, node }. "This set's nodes plus the edges among them" is built in plain code today:

JS
const inSet = new Set(
  memberships.map((m) => `${m.set}/${m.node}`));
const setMembers = [
  ...memberships.map(({ set, node }) =>
    ({ set, member: node })),
  ...sets.flatMap((set) => edges
    .filter((e) =>
      inSet.has(`${set}/${e.source}`) &&
      inSet.has(`${set}/${e.target}`))
    .map((e) => ({ set, member: e.id }))),
];
Python
in_set = {(m["set"], m["node"])
          for m in memberships}
set_members = [
    {"set": m["set"], "member": m["node"]}
    for m in memberships
] + [
    {"set": s, "member": e["id"]}
    for s in sets for e in edges
    if (s, e["source"]) in in_set
    and (s, e["target"]) in in_set
]

The edge half alone can be written with existing operators: chart(edges).flow(join(sourceSets, { on: "source" }), join(targetSets, { on: "target" }), filter((d) => d.sourceSet === d.targetSet)), where sourceSets and targetSets are memberships with its columns renamed. That is awkward, and it still cannot be put together with the node rows, because a flow has no operator that appends one table's rows to another's. So three things are missing, each with a small fix: resolve takes a list of selections in from (the nodes and the edges are two layers); the edges need a key column (id) so rows can refer to them; and enclose needs the members option of decision 9. The plain-code table is the honest spelling until a flow can append rows.

What it needs from #974

#974 plans one geometry query per file, with a boundary query (a node's own outline) that enters only when something first needs it. Edges as members are that first user: the blob needs a line mark's geometry (its path and stroke width), not just its box or center, both to grow it by padding and to start spanning-tree links at the closest point. The prototype reads straight segments; a curved edge would need its path as points.

Box and hull

Members are just shapes, so Shape.box() and Shape.hull() contain line members too, through the same query. When both ends of an edge are members, the edge already lies inside, since both shapes are convex; it matters only for an edge whose end is outside the set.

What via did that this does not

Two things, neither needed by an example. via could make the outline follow a route it does not draw, but a route the outline follows is inside it at the padding anyway, so it might as well be a member; a route with no mark can be drawn as an invisible line. And via drew each edge as a tube as wide as the node bulge, while a member edge is slim (its stroke plus padding); a wider look comes from padding or convexity. So via can be dropped. With it gone, avoiding is the only strategy parameter that holds refs. It could later become a second operand role of enclose (the nodes to keep out, beside the members); that is not redesigned here.

(f) Munkres' three-point topology

Munkres, Topology (2nd ed., §12), draws the nine topologies on {a, b, c}. In the last panel the {a, c} set wraps a and c with an even margin and joins them with a thick band under b. The GoFish port's {a, c} neighborhood is today a hand-transcribed Bézier path (#717, concave hulls for enclose). The whole set and the pairs {a, b} and {b, c} are ellipses through the custom-shape function. 

layer([a, b, c]).relate(({ a, b, c }) => [
  enclose({ shape: oval, padding: 30 }, [a, b, c]),
  enclose({ shape: oval, padding: 16 }, [a, b]),
  enclose({ shape: oval, padding: 16 }, [b, c]),
  enclose({ shape: Shape.spine({ avoiding: [b], neck, roundness,
                                 clearance: 6 }),
            padding: 22 }, [a, c]),
])

Matched: even margins around a and c, a thick band, and smooth joins. Still different: the bottom of the band is a wide V rather than Munkres' flatter bottom, because the link is the shortest path around b. A link rule that bends the band to follow b's outline at a fixed gap would get closer, and is a change to routing rather than a new parameter.

The hull preset is within half a pixel

Before rounding the hull shortcut is exact: with nothing avoided, every Delaunay triangle is filled and every hull edge is a tube as wide as the bulge, so the union is the convex hull grown by the bulge. Rounding keeps the circles and the straight sides where they are and changes only the joins between them, where the curvature steps from 1/R to 0. Measured against a true padded convex hull (black line), the outline is within 0.2 px of it with roundness equal to padding (U and Gentoo), and within 0.3 px at roundness: 15, the most the U allows.

The spine shortcut draws exactly the members and the routed spanning tree, rounded. At convexity: 0 alpha is 0, so no triangle is filled and no extra edge is added, and since there is no closing, nothing else is filled either.

The curvature never jumps

Future work: placing points and regions together

Not part of this proposal: the Munkres example may be drawn best by placing the points and the region together, with an energy that pushes shapes apart and rewards symmetry, as Penrose and Keenan Crane's work do. Every strategy here draws around shapes that are already placed, so that would be a change to layout, not a new shape.

Part 2 · Context

Josh's tensions, answered

Relational mark or operator?

What decides it is the kind of operands, not the construct (#707, one combinator indexed by operand kind): fresh children behave like an operator, refs like a connector, a mix is an open term. enclose is used all three ways today, so it stays one construct. Over fresh children the combinator form is the component: it lays out its children, has a box, can be named and pointed at. A user-supplied drawn shape, as in Bluefish's callback, is the #1101 function escape hatch.

A SwiftUI modifier?

SwiftUI's .background decorates one view. enclose(opts, [node]) already does that, so a .background() modifier would be exact sugar, and on an operator it could give one background per group like .label() (#750). It cannot reach nodes that live in different places (Topology, hyperedges, one species across lakes). Kept as the runner-up.

Containment or connection?

Both, and the literature says so: the set survey files Bubble Sets as regions, the hypergraph survey as node-link, and KelpFusion is one dial from spanning tree to convex hull. So the design keeps it in the relational-mark family (with line, ribbon), makes connection versus containment one dial (convexity) on one member, and lets real connections, such as a graph's edges, be members themselves.

Where it lives: three approaches

How the strategies are spelled is a separate axis (next section); all three approaches use shape: Shape.*.

Approach A: enclose becomes a relational mark recommended

Rebuilt with createRelationalMark instead of createNodeOperator. Keeps the combinator form for fresh children, refs or a mix; gains the bag form, fusion, the #752 split, per-group paint and the paint-below default. The component case is unchanged except for the corner option:

How it composes. enclose(opts, operands) is layer(operands) plus one outline node computed after the operands are placed, the framing #713 already uses ("enclose = layer + hull ink"). The outline is computed from each operand's boundary (#974, a geometry query for a node's outline as points; box corners when a mark has no better answer). No new core node kind. Its box is the box of the drawn outline (#707's ink rule). Under animation the outline is recomputed each frame; how it should tween is out of scope.

Overlap check. layer: exact composition. Constraint.nest with a rect outer, and rect plus align span: agree with box mode in degenerate cases; build box mode through the same code as nest. line/ribbon: a two-member spine looks like a thick line, degenerate. subtract: not an overlap. pack: its enclosing circle is a possible future Shape.circle().

Trade-offs. One name, one mental model, every hard example expressible. New terms: Shape, blob, hull, spine, convexity, roundness, neck, avoiding, clearance. Awkward: the per-group background behind each lake needs chart(selectAll("bars")).flow(group({ by: "lake" })).mark(enclose()), longer than a modifier.

Approach B: a .background() modifier only (SwiftUI)

text("H").background({ padding: 4, fill: "#ffe8a3" })

  .flow(spread({ by: "lake", dir: "x" })
          .background({ padding: 8, fill: "#f0f1f3" }),
        spread({ by: "species", dir: "x", spacing: 9 }))
  .mark(rect({ h: "count", fill: "species" }))

Desugars to the enclose combinator around the node. Short for one-node decorations and per-group panels. Fails every hard example: the Topology set, hyperedges and the trout set are not single nodes of the tree. It would still need enclose for refs, so it is a second spelling, not a replacement.

Approach C: A plus the modifier as sugar runner-up

Everything in A, plus x.background(opts) = enclose(opts, [x]) (and per group on operators). Allowed as exact composition, but one concept gets two names. What would make it the pick: real examples where per-node or per-group backgrounds inside a flow are the common case, such as label pills, facet panel backgrounds and zebra stripes.

How strategies and parameters are spelled

S1 · shape: Shape.* recommended

One option with a family; padding and paint stay on enclose; each strategy carries only the parameters it uses. Default Shape.box(), today's behavior.

S2 · box() / hull() / spine()

One function per strategy, as in ggforce. Splits one concept into three operators, against "one concept is one operator" and the #397/#717 direction.

S3 · shape: "hull" + flat options

Strings with convexity and avoiding on enclose (#717 comment). Rejected by #1013: a family with arguments is a namespace of calls, and flat options would be accepted and ignored by strategies that do not use them.

Precedent

Globs (Steve Ruiz)

"A globs-based vector editor" (globs.design, source), by the author of perfect-freehand and perfect-arrows, which GoFish already uses. It implements Andrew Glassner's glob ("Globs: A Primitive Shape for Graceful Blends Between Circles", JCGT 4(3), 2015): two circles of any radii joined by a neck of two Bézier curves that leave each circle along a tangent, shaped by four handles. You place nodes, connect them with globs, and drag handles; a glob follows its nodes.

Compared with the blob: globs are hand-placed pairs; the blob computes every neck for any number of members, fills between them, and keeps non-members out. Glassner's neck meets the circle along a tangent (G1); his page does not claim curvature continuity, while the blob is G2 everywhere. The shared look is round ends and necks that narrow and widen gracefully. A glob is a candidate output for the #1101 function, and its neck handles are a reference for a better link rule in the Munkres panel.

What GoFish should take: one name with a shape value (SwiftUI, Compose, TikZ), a relation over refs that also nests (Bluefish), a shared padding plus one knob per strategy (ggforce), members, non-members and real edges as the blob's inputs (bubblesets-js). Names. box matches TikZ and ggforce's rect; hull matches Plot, ggforce, d3 and the roadmap. No library says "spine"; Bubble Sets and Josh's #717 comment say "bubble". spine names the connecting skeleton and reads well over a graph ("a spine along these edges"). For the general member, kelp means little to most readers, bubble names a different algorithm, region collides with the region-compositing operators, outline is a candidate option key, and set shadows JavaScript's Set; blob is an everyday word and Bluefish's name for a hand-drawn set outline.

The knobs. ggforce's and concaveman's concavity grows more convex; alpha is a radius in CGAL and an inverse radius in Python's alphashape. convexity reads its direction from the name and has no unit. roundness is a pixel length, bigger is rounder; it is the plain word for the property, and its unit matches the pixel corner radius of CSS (border-radius), Vega-Lite (cornerRadius) and Figma. It is not called smoothing, because smoothing on noise() and the old blob knob both change what is filled, and roundness never does. neck is the everyday word for the narrow join, and also Glassner's word for the part of a glob between its circles.

Part 3 · History

Earlier versions and retired decisions

Oldest first. Nothing here is part of the current proposal.

v1: three strategies, with the alpha shape

Three members: Shape.box(), Shape.hull({ alpha }) (convex, or concave as an alpha shape with a pixel radius), and Shape.spine({ avoiding, via }) built as a bubble set: connect the members with a tree of lines that bends around non-members, then draw a smooth outline at a fixed distance around it.

The concave hull's dent came from the members' spacing and ignored the gray dots; the spine's dent came from the avoiding list. On the Topology set only the spine kept b out:

S1', the three-member spelling on a shared kernel, was the pick of v1 and v2. Hull and spine had no common dial, the middle (filled where dense, links where sparse) needed a fourth member, and a raw alpha brought a pixel length whose direction libraries disagree on.

v2: KelpFusion and bubble sets compared

Josh asked whether KelpFusion has the convex hull as a special case, and how it compares to a bubble set. Sources: Meulemans et al., "KelpFusion: A Hybrid Set Visualization Technique", IEEE TVCG 19(11), 2013 (author copy); Collins, Penn, Carpendale, "Bubble Sets", IEEE TVCG 15(6), 2009 (author copy).

How KelpFusion is built

  1. Connect the members. For every pair, the candidate edge is the shortest route clear of every other node's allocation area (a disc of radius r cut to the node's Voronoi cell, the part of the plane closer to it than to any other node). It keeps candidates by a shortest-path graph with parameter t ≥ 1: an edge weighs its length to the power t and is kept only if no other path is lighter. t = 1 keeps everything (the convex hull); t → ∞ gives the minimum spanning tree.
  2. Fill some faces: a face is filled if it is small enough (area per node below A), holds no non-member, and does not clash with a set drawn beneath.
  3. Draw the union of discs, edges of width w and filled faces; smooth; cut out non-member areas.

The convex hull is an exact special case for the graph, but the drawn shape is the hull only when every face can be filled and no non-member is inside, and even then it is not the hull grown evenly by a padding.

How a bubble set is built

Members are joined by virtual edges bent around non-member boxes; members and edges add energy and non-members subtract; the outline is an isocontour, with the threshold lowered until every member is inside. The paper says it "cannot guarantee that non-set member items will be excluded".

Side by side

KelpFusion at t = ∞, 2, 1.5, 1, then the convex hull and a bubble set. Story-local KelpFusion with A = ∞, no Voronoi cut, straight-sided faces, no smoothing.

In the paper's study (13 people, four overlapping sets on a map), KelpFusion scored 86.5% accuracy against 54.5% for Bubble Sets, and was faster. The conclusion then: KelpFusion overlapped hull and spine in part and replaced neither, which led to v3.

v3: the generalized KelpFusion (Shape.blob with density)

Josh asked whether KelpFusion could be generalized, with configurable circle sizes, to cover the usual hull, and whether it and the alpha shape could be unified. v3 introduced Shape.blob({ density, neck, smoothing, avoiding, via }) with hull and spine as shortcuts. It picked links by KelpFusion's shortest-path graph at t = 1 / density, filled every closed face without an avoided node, and smoothed only by closing.

Alpha shape and KelpFusion share a pipeline (keep some Delaunay links, fill some faces, grow the result, end at the convex hull) but not a parameter: the alpha hull is exactly the closing of the points by a disc of radius alpha (Edelsbrunner, Kirkpatrick, Seidel 1983; the match with closing is my derivation), its complex falls apart below half the widest gap and changes with scale, while KelpFusion's graph always stays connected and does not change with scale; and KelpFusion keeps long links without their triangles, which no alpha complex does.

Josh's review of v3: alpha shapes "still seem to have a nice aesthetic to them because they're a bit more convex than the kelp comparisons"; the Munkres outline "still feels too shrinkwrapped"; and "I don't want to see a shape curvature change when the circle connects to the link." v3's outline was circle arcs, straight sides and closing arcs, so its curvature jumped at every join.

v4: the move to the current construction, and the rejected curve key

v4 made three changes, which are the current construction: blur the region and take its level ½; replace density with convexity (the alpha fill rule on a unitless scale, plus a spanning tree); and draw links that bound a filled triangle at full width. density's middle (long links kept without their triangles) is no longer reachable; no example needs it.

The comparison grids below put the classic alpha shape and v3 beside the new construction. Josh's verdict: the classic alpha shape fits the data worse than the new construction.

Three ways to get a curvature-continuous outline

(a) A convolution surface: add up a Gaussian bump along the skeleton and draw one iso-contour (the curve where the summed field equals a level). Where pieces meet their fields add, so the outline swells past the promised padding. Out. (b) A closed curve through the v3 outline: GoFish's data-space curves interpolate y(x) and its connector curves join two endpoints, and catmullRom is open and G1, so the prototype tried closed Catmull-Rom (G1), a periodic natural cubic spline (C2) and a Yuksel-style C2 blend of circles (Yuksel, ACM TOG 2020, listed in #957; my own variant). (c) Blur the region: chosen.

The curve key, rejected. enclose({ shape: Shape.hull(), curve: Curve.<x>() }) would reuse the relational marks' key, but no Curve member is closed, a curve through outline points breaks padding and clearance, and it would be a second smoothing control. The findings go to #957: the closed Yuksel-style blend is easy to build and C2, and it ripples where point spacing is uneven.

v4 also tried treating an avoided node inside the set as a wall during the closing, so the band wrapped b in a U. Dropped; see the retired decisions.

v5: via, replaced by edges as members

v5 took a graph's edges as a strategy parameter, Shape.blob({ via: selectAll("edges") }): each listed edge between two members was a link, always kept and never rerouted. It was replaced because the edges can simply be members, which removes a strategy parameter that holds refs and draws the spine without links the graph does not have. 

enclose({ members: "node",
          shape: Shape.blob({ convexity, smoothing,
            via: selectAll("edges"),
            avoiding: selectAll("nodes"), clearance: 3 }),
          padding: 6 })

v5 also showed the Gentoo species alone, with the other two avoided and cropped to its cluster; the penguins tab now shows all three species.

v6: smoothing, replaced by roundness

Josh: convexity and smoothing overlapped; both fill gaps. Until v6 the blob had a closing step and a different rule for avoided nodes.

  • A triangle was filled only when no avoided node's keep-out zone touched it, so an avoided node inside the set left a hole shaped like the triangles around it.
  • The union of the pieces was closed by a disc of radius smoothing, which filled gaps narrower than about twice that radius.
  • The blur width was σ = ½ min(smoothing, thinnest piece).

So smoothing filled gaps the way convexity does, and it also reshaped the holes. Small values left triangle holes, middle values half-rounded them, and only large values filled the triangles back in and cut just the circles. "Why these knobs are orthogonal" measures the overlap. v7: the same outline, computed in milliseconds

Josh: the computation was too slow (122 ms per slider step on the penguins). v6 rasterized the region on a 0.5 px grid, blurred it, and checked the topology with more blurs, so its cost grew with the area of the drawing. v7 keeps the knobs and the promises and changes how the outline is computed (construction steps 4 to 6): the region's outline is traced exactly from closed-form distances, and it is rounded by smoothing along its own length, with circles held in place. So the cost grows with the length of the outline, and a change of roundness reruns only the rounding. A Gaussian along the outline cannot join two pieces or open a hole, so v6's topology check became a check that loops do not cross. Two rules were added for what the blur did for free: an inlet narrower than 1.5 σ is closed before smoothing, and the outline is kept out of every keep-out zone by a smooth maximum. Routed links now follow the avoided nodes' keep-out circles exactly where they wrap them.

What changed in the drawings: the band under b in the Topology example now hugs b's ring (gap 7.6 px for 6 asked, was 11.0), and its joins there turn over about 10 px instead of 20; holes are exact circles to 0.001 px (were within 0.7 px); the hull preset is within 0.3 px of the true padded hull (was 0.8 px outside); and rounding now stops at the smallest circle in the region, keep-out circles and box corners included, so the trout bars' corners round less at 8 px than before (the cap is their 4 px corner radius). Speed in the playground: penguins about 11 ms per slider step, the other datasets 1 to 3 ms.

Retired decisions
  • smoothing on Shape.blob() (v6). Replaced by roundness. Josh: convexity and smoothing overlapped; both fill gaps.
  • Leave out triangles that touch an avoided node (v6). Replaced by filling them and cutting the node's clearance circle out, so an avoided node inside the set is always a round hole.
  • via on Shape.blob() (v5). Replaced by edges as members (open decision 13).
  • alpha as a pixel radius on Shape.hull() (v1). Retired in v3; alpha came back in v4 as convexity.
  • A shared kernel for three members, with Shape.kelp({ t }) left out (v1). Retired in v3, when hull and spine became shortcuts of one member.
  • Leave alpha out (v3). Retired in v4: alpha is back, as convexity.
  • density as the blob's dial (v3). Dropped in v4 for convexity.
  • An avoided node inside the set as a wall during the closing (v4 decision 13). Retired. Josh: creates a cusp (a point where the outline turns sharply).
  • A curve key on enclose (v4). Rejected; open decision 12 asks Josh to confirm.
